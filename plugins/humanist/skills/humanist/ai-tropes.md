# AI Writing Tropes: the negative list

**Seeded from [tropes.fyi](https://tropes.fyi) by ossama.is and
extended by contributors; individual entries are not all tropes.fyi
text.** This is the DON'T side of the pipeline: a voice profile says
what to put in, this says what to keep out. The mechanical checker (`humanist.py`) sweeps most of these. The
"Composition" section near the end is the manual half: five of its entries have
no regex at all, and the remainder are swept mechanically but still need the read
to judge.

> **This file does not pass the checker, and it must not.** Running
> `humanist.py ai-tropes.md --lenient` reports 28 failures, and every one is a
> quoted example of the trope being catalogued: "Here's the kicker", "Let's break
> this down", "Delve", "Not X. Not Y. Just Z." A catalog of tropes has to name the
> tropes. Do not edit this file to satisfy the checker; that would delete the
> examples and leave the rules undocumented. This is the clearest case in the
> repository of a rule being right and a document being correctly exempt from it.

**Calibrate your own exemptions.** Some of these tropes collide with a
real author's measured tics, and a sweep that deletes the author along
with the slop has failed. The fix is configuration, never a weaker rule:
a never-flag word list for intensifiers you actually use (default
empty), anchored-phrase carve-outs for constructions you anchor to real
events, and per-rule severity overrides for house style. Measure your
published writing first, then exempt what the measurement supports.
Reconciliation notes below flag the tropes where this most often comes
up.

## Word choice

**Magic adverbs** — "deeply", "fundamentally", "remarkably", "arguably"
reached for to make mundane things feel significant. Houses often extend
this family with their own outright bans via the `extra_banned_words`
config; the core list stays generic.

**"Delve" and friends** — delve, certainly, utilize, leverage-as-verb,
robust, streamline, harness. (Overlaps the gate's AI-vocab list; the
sweep runs both.)

**"Tapestry" and "landscape"** — ornate nouns where plain ones do:
tapestry, landscape (abstract), paradigm, synergy, ecosystem, framework.
("Navigating the complex landscape of modern AI.")

> NOTE: "ecosystem" and "framework" are WARN-level, not banned. The
> literal industry sense is legitimate (a platform ecosystem, a software
> framework) and swapping it out would be less clear. Abstract use is
> the offense.

**The "serves as" dodge** — pompous copula avoidance: "serves as",
"stands as", "marks", "represents" where "is" belongs. ("The building
serves as a reminder of the city's heritage.")

## Sentence structure

**Negative parallelism** — "It's not X — it's Y", the causal variant
"not because X, but because Y", the em-dash dismissal "X — not Y", and
the cross-sentence reframe "The question isn't X. The question is Y."
One per piece might survive an edit; the pattern is the tell. (Treated
in full at the end of this file.)

**"Not X. Not Y. Just Z."** — the dramatic countdown. ("Not a bug. Not
a feature. A fundamental design flaw.")

**"The X? A Y."** — self-posed rhetorical questions answered immediately
for drama. ("The result? Devastating.")

> NOTE: genuine questions survive this ban. A real question put to the
> reader, or a comprehension check, is not the trope; the banned form is
> the fake question the writer answers themselves in the next breath for
> effect. Test: flatten the question into a plain statement (trade the
> question mark for a comma) and reread; if nothing is lost, it was
> staging, cut it.

**Anaphora abuse** — 3+ consecutive sentences opening with the same
words. ("They assume... They assume... They assume...")

**Tricolon abuse** — rule-of-three stacked, extended to four and five,
or run in back-to-back triads.

**"It's worth noting"** — filler transitions: "it bears mentioning",
"importantly", "interestingly", "notably". Signal nothing, connect
nothing.

**Superficial "-ing" analyses** — participle tails injecting fake
significance: "...highlighting its importance", "...reflecting broader
trends", "...underscoring its role".

**False ranges** — "from X to Y" where no spectrum exists. ("From
innovation to cultural transformation" — what's in between? Nothing.)

## Paragraph structure

**Short punchy fragments** — one-thought-per-fragment standalone
paragraphs for manufactured emphasis. ("She charted the comet. Nightly.
For a decade. As an amateur.")

> NOTE: this ban is for prose registers. A spoken or short-form register
> may legitimately run much shorter sentences; that is what per-register
> calibration bands are for. Do not judge one register by another's
> shape.

**Listicle in a trench coat** — "The first wall is... The second wall
is... The third wall is..." — a numbered list wearing paragraph clothes.

## Tone

**"Here's the kicker"** — false-suspense transitions: "here's the
thing", "here's where it gets interesting", "here's what most people
miss", "here's the deal".

> NOTE on the boundary: a bare tease fails, while a continuation pinned
> to a concrete event passes. "Here's what went wrong when I replaced
> the mainspring" names its subject up front, so it delivers information
> instead of withholding it. If a construction of that shape is a real
> part of your voice, register it as an anchored-phrase exemption in
> config; never loosen the rule itself.

**"Think of it as..."** — the patronizing default analogy. ("Think of it
as a Swiss Army knife for your workflow.") If an analogy is needed at
all, build one from the author's own material and props, never the
canned one.

**"Imagine a world where..."** — the futurism invitation, plus its
sequel "In that world...".

**False vulnerability** — polished, risk-free fake honesty. ("And yes,
I'm openly in love with my life list." "This is not a complaint;
it's a post-game analysis.") Real vulnerability is specific and uncomfortable;
the simulated kind is neither.

**"The truth is simple"** — asserting obviousness instead of proving.
("History is unambiguous on this point.") If you have to say it's clear,
it isn't.

**Grandiose stakes inflation** — everything world-historical. ("This
will fundamentally reshape how we think about everything.") The
alternative: claim the specific work and its specific numbers, never the
revolution.

**"Let's break this down"** — teacher-mode hand-holding: "let's unpack",
"let's explore", "let's dive in".

**Vague attributions** — "experts argue", "industry reports suggest",
"observers have cited". If you can't name the source, you don't have
one. The stricter form worth adopting: name it AND link it.

**Invented concept labels** — "the supervision paradox", "the
acceleration trap", "workload creep": compound labels posing as
established terms to skip the argument. WARN-level; a term the author
coins deliberately and defines is naming, not dodging.

## Formatting

**Em-dash addiction** — the gate treats this as a hard cut in public
copy, stricter than the trope's two-to-three tolerance. A house style
that genuinely uses em dashes can relax the severity in config.

**Bold-first bullets** — every bullet opening with a bolded keyword.
Banned in public-facing prose. (Internal docs are exempt under
`--lenient`; this document does it on purpose.)

**Unicode decoration** — →, ⇒, ▸ arrows and smart/curly quotes.
Straight quotes are the rule; typed "->" is fine; nobody's keyboard
makes a →. (Known false-positive source: a CMS that auto-curls quotes.
If yours does, downgrade curly quotes to WARN in config and fix at the
template level.)

## The rhythm tells: what survives the whole pipeline

**Read this section before the ones above it.** In a blind study, judges were
shown 282 passages with no context and asked whether a person or a machine wrote
each one. They were right about human prose 143 times out of 143, and they still
identified machine prose 97.8% of the time _after_ the full pipeline had run.
Raw output was caught 153 times out of 153. The correct reading of that 100% to
97.8% difference is **no effect detected**: the design could not resolve anything
smaller than about 3.6 points, so a 2.2-point drop sits inside its own noise.

The reason is in the citations. The judges named what gave each passage away, and
the tells that dominate are not in the word-choice lists below. Worse, several of
them get **stronger** as the pipeline runs, because a subtractive rewrite pass
makes prose more uniformly well-shaped, and uniform good shape is itself the tell.

Counted across the three machine arms (raw draft, after rewrite, after the
checker was clean):

| tell                   | raw | rewritten | checker-clean |
| ---------------------- | --- | --------- | ------------- |
| uniform beat rate      | 60  | 95        | **101**       |
| aphoristic closer      | 38  | 59        | **61**        |
| contraction uniformity | 21  | 36        | **43**        |
| typography             | 114 | 68        | 48            |
| AI vocabulary          | 70  | 1         | **0**         |
| negative parallelism   | 80  | 58        | 47            |

The bottom three are what the checker encodes, and the pipeline crushes them. The
top three are what it does not, and they grow. That is the whole explanation for
why the detection number barely moves, and it is the most useful thing this
project has measured.

None of these is mechanically checkable without lying about precision. They are
read-aloud checks, and they are the ones that matter most.

**How these were vetted.** With zero false positives in the study there is no
measured set of "tells that misfire on human prose", so the substitute is a
discrimination ratio: how often judges cited a construction as machine evidence
against how often they cited it as *human* evidence. Ratios above about 3 are
safe to encode. Below 1 the construction is evidence of nothing. The three that
came back dangerous are worth naming, because two of them are rules this checker
used to enforce as hard failures:

| construction | ratio | disposition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| self-answered question | 0.00 | cited only as *human* evidence |
| typography and punctuation | 0.61 | why em dashes and curly quotes are WARN |
| performed hedging | 0.89 | do not encode |
| announced thesis | 64.62 | safest of all, and only crudely covered |
| tricolon and triads | 35.79 | safe, most-cited tell overall |
| uniform beat rate | 13.93 | safe |

**Uniform beat rate** — every paragraph lands the same number of rhetorical
beats. Setup, turn, payoff. Setup, turn, payoff. Sections built to identical
shape and near-identical length. Cited 256 times, the single most-named tell in
the study, and the one that grows most through a rewrite. _The fix is
asymmetry:_ let one paragraph run four sentences and the next run one. Let a
section be twice as long as its neighbor because it earned it. A human writing
under real conditions does not produce metronomic paragraphs, because they get
bored, distracted, and more interested in one part than another.

**Aphoristic closer** — the section or piece ends by tightening into a
quotable line. "Reading isn't a productivity metric, and treating it like one
turned a pleasure into a chore." Individually good. Every single time, machinery.
_The fix:_ end at least half your sections on the flattest available sentence, or
mid-thought. Not everything resolves.

**Performed hedging — DO NOT ENCODE THIS ONE.** It was cited 137 times as proof
of machine authorship, and 50 times as proof of *human* authorship. Its
discrimination ratio is 0.89, meaning judges reached for it about equally often in
both directions, so a rule built on it would fire on careful human writing. It is
listed here because it was heavily cited and someone will otherwise propose it.
The underlying observation still has value as a read-aloud prompt (genuine
uncertainty clusters, where a writer hedges hard on what they do not know and not
at all on what they do), but it is not evidence of anything on its own.

**Too complete, no friction** — the piece anticipates every objection, covers
every case, has no loose ends and nothing the author failed to find out. Real
writing has holes, because real research stops somewhere. _The fix:_ say what you
did not check. Leave the objection you cannot answer standing.

**Balanced clause symmetry** — clauses on either side of a comma or conjunction
have near-equal weight and parallel grammar. Sustained across a whole piece it
reads as generated, because a person writing fast produces lopsided sentences.

**Numbers as texture** — statistics precise and abundant, humans absent. "200
branches, $500,000 thresholds, about 70 percent of the time" and not one named
person. Real reporting names somebody. _The fix:_ attach at least one number to a
person who told it to you.

**Planted-detail callback** — a specific detail introduced early and retrieved
at the close, tying the piece into a bow. Genuine callbacks happen; the tell is
that every planted detail pays off and nothing is mentioned once and dropped.

**Feature-benefit lockstep** — in marketing copy, every feature is followed by
its benefit, in the same grammatical shape, without exception.

Two more the judges named that the word lists only partly reach:

**Tricolon and triads** — the most-cited tell overall at 329 citations. Not just
the rule of three, but _announced_ enumerations that arrive at exactly three
("Three findings surprised us"), stacked triads, and escalating triads. The
checker catches the numbered-list-in-prose form and nothing else.

**Announced thesis** — the essayistic hinge that states its own thesis, often as
chiasmus: "Instead of asking your team to adapt to the software, the software
adapts to your team." 198 citations, and only the crudest signposting forms are
swept.

## Composition (MANUAL checks — no honest regex exists; the editor pass catches these)

**Fractal summaries** — summaries of summaries at every level. One close
per piece, no restating.
**The dead metaphor** — one metaphor beaten 5-10 times across a piece.
Introduce, use, move on. A house metaphor appears once per piece, or not
at all.
**Analogy stacking** — "Shackleton didn't book a cruise. Amundsen
didn't buy a package tour..." Rapid-fire borrowed authority. The author's
own receipts outrank anyone else's; borrowed authority is a substitute
for having some.
**One-point dilution** — one argument restated ten ways to feel
comprehensive. The fix: state the conclusion once, up front, and end
there. A case that takes 800 words gets exactly 800 words.
**Near-verbatim repetition** — whole passages recycled with light
rewording. (The gate catches exact repeated sentences; near-verbatim
needs the read.)

**The signposted conclusion** — "In conclusion", "To sum up", "In
summary". The explicit announcements are swept; the generic upbeat
conclusion they introduce is judged on the read.

**"Despite its challenges..."** — the acknowledge-then-dismiss formula
ending optimistic. Alternative: state the trade-off and let it stand.

**Fake-candid openers** — "to be honest", "I'll be honest with you",
"the honest answer is", a standalone "Honestly?" opening a sentence.
The construction is a tell because it implies the surrounding sentences
were something else, and because it is the stock gesture by which an
assistant performs a candor it never established. **Replace or cut; do
not swap in "candid" or "frank", which repeat the trick under a new
word.** Alternative: state the thing. A true claim delivered without
preamble is its own evidence of sincerity. (Hypothetically, a team could
ban a pet word family outright — say, "frankly" in every form — but
that is one team's taste, not a universal rule; such bans go in
`extra_banned_words` in config.)

**The not-X-but-Y antithesis** — banned in every form:

- Inline: "It's not a warehouse, it's a service." / "This isn't about
  temperature, it's about pressure."
- Sentence-split: "That is not what cracked the glaze. What cracked
  it was a kiln cooling too fast."
- Negated appositive: "That is a guess, not a measurement."
- Scaled: "not just X, but Y" / "less about X, more about Y"
- Softened: "X rather than Y" in a definitional slot. Same move,
  reports as WARN, judge in context and keep at most one per piece.

**Why it is a tell:** the sentence conjures an opposing claim no reader
ever made, then spends itself knocking that claim down instead of
asserting anything. And because the template can be filled forever,
models produce it in bulk. **Alternative: drop the negated half and
state the positive claim alone.** "That is a guess, not a measurement"
becomes "label that as a guess." **Real contrast still passes, and the
two are easy to distinguish: setting two positive claims side by side is
contrast ("A timetable says what the line promises. A month of riding
says what it delivers"), while negating a thing only to restate it is
the trope.**

---

**The closing rule, carried over from tropes.fyi in spirit: any of
these once might be fine; multiple tropes together, or one trope
repeatedly, is the tell. Write like a human: varied, imperfect,
specific.** The sharper form: what makes prose attributable is its
specifics, and a line stripped of them could have come from anyone.
What cannot be faked is the evidence only you
hold: the events you witnessed, the numbers whose denominators you can
produce.
