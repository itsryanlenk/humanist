# Why I Stopped Setting Reading Goals

For three years I set a reading goal every January. Fifty books, then sixty, then seventy-five. I tracked them in a spreadsheet, color-coded by genre, and I hit the number every single year. Then, last December, I realized I could not remember the plot of a single book I had read in October.

The problem was not the books. The problem was the metric. When you measure reading by volume, you optimize for volume: shorter books, faster pacing, fewer pauses to think. I started choosing novels the way a commuter chooses a route, by how quickly they would get me to the end. Reading became a task to complete rather than an experience to have.

There is a deeper issue here, too. A goal like "read fifty books" carries a hidden assumption: that more reading is always better reading. But the value of a book is not in finishing it. It is in what it changes about how you see the world afterward. Some of the most important books in my life took me months to get through, and I abandoned others halfway because they had already given me what I needed.

So this year I set no number. Instead, I keep a single page of notes for every book, written a week after I finish it. If I cannot fill the page, the book did not stay with me, and that is information worth having. Some months I read one book. Some months I read four. The spreadsheet is gone.

What I have noticed is that I am reading harder books now, and I am reading them more slowly. I stop to look things up. I reread chapters. I put a book down for a week and come back to it. None of that would have fit inside a seventy-five-book year.

Reading is not a productivity metric, and treating it like one turned a pleasure into a chore. The books were never the point. The thinking was.
